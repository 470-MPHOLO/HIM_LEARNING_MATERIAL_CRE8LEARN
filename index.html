Welcome, HIM students. You are training to be a guardian of the medical record, not a doctor or nurse. You will translate clinical documentation into standardised codes (such as ICD-10) for billing, research and patient safety.

So study Anatomy and Physiology through pathology (disease) and medical terminology: how the body is built, what happens when it breaks, and how to spell it correctly.

One rule runs through every section. A coder codes what the provider has documented. You do not diagnose from symptoms or test results. When documentation is unclear or incomplete, you query the provider.

Which coding system? Laterality, combination codes and initial/subsequent encounters are features of ICD-10-CM. The WHO ICD-10 works differently in places. Confirm with your lecturer which system your course uses.

1. The Nervous System (Tortora Ch. 12–15)

The nervous system is the body's electrical wiring. It is divided into the Central Nervous System (CNS) (brain and spinal cord) and the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS) (the nerves branching out).

Core concepts

  • The neuron: The basic cell of the nervous system. Dendrites receive signals and the axon sends them.
  • The Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): The involuntary part. Sympathetic is fight or flight; parasympathetic is rest and digest.
HIM FOCUS: PATHOLOGIES & TERMINOLOGY
  • Cerebrovascular accident (CVA): A stroke. Look for the site (e.g. middle cerebral artery) and the type: blockage (ischaemic) or bleed (haemorrhagic).
  • Transient ischaemic attack (TIA): A "mini-stroke" in which blood flow is briefly blocked. Symptoms resolve, but it is a critical warning sign.
  • Neuropathy: Disease of the nerves, often seen as a complication of diabetes.
  • Meningitis: Inflammation of the meninges, the protective coverings of the brain and spinal cord. Usually caused by bacteria or viruses; fungal causes also occur.
  • Epilepsy: A seizure disorder. Charts distinguish seizures starting in one area of the brain (focal, formerly called partial) from those involving both sides (generalised). You will meet both terms.
  • Multiple sclerosis (MS): An autoimmune disease in which the body attacks the myelin sheath (the protective covering) of nerves in the CNS.
CHECKPOINT QUESTION

Scenario: A patient is admitted with "Meningitis due to Streptococcus pneumoniae." Which structure is inflamed, and why does the named organism matter for coding?

Answer: The meninges are inflamed. The organism matters because ICD-10 classifies bacterial meningitis by cause, so the organism determines the code, which supports accurate statistics and public health tracking.

2. The Musculoskeletal System (Tortora Ch. 6–11)

The system of structure, support and movement: bones, joints and muscles.

Core concepts

  • Bone cells: Osteoblasts build new bone and osteoclasts break down old bone. They must stay in balance.
  • Joints: Where two bones meet. Synovial joints (knee, shoulder) are freely movable.
HIM FOCUS: PATHOLOGIES & TERMINOLOGY
  • Fractures: A very common musculoskeletal diagnosis. Coders must pay attention to:
    • Type: Closed/simple (bone breaks, skin intact) versus open/compound (bone breaks through the skin). Under ICD-10-CM conventions, a fracture not documented as open or closed is classified as closed, so read the provider's wording carefully.
    • Pattern: Comminuted (three or more pieces), spiral (twisting), greenstick (incomplete break, common in children).
    • Laterality: Left versus right matters for coding.
    • Encounter: Initial (active treatment) or subsequent (routine healing or follow-up care).
  • Osteoarthritis (OA): "Wear and tear." Mainly degenerative loss of joint cartilage, commonly in weight-bearing joints (knees, hips) but also the hands.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): An autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the joint lining. It is systemic and can affect the whole body. Never confuse OA with RA in coding.
  • Osteoporosis: "Porous bone." Bone is broken down faster than it is rebuilt, leaving it brittle and prone to fracture.
CHECKPOINT QUESTION

Scenario: The physician documents: "Fell off a ladder. Right femur is broken and protruding through the skin." What three pieces of information must you extract for accurate coding, and what else do you need to confirm?

Answer: 1. The bone (femur). 2. The laterality (right). 3. The fracture type (open/compound, because it protrudes through the skin). You also need the encounter type (initial or subsequent). If anything is missing from the documentation, you query the provider.

3. The Senses (Tortora Ch. 16 & 17)

We focus on the two most frequently coded sensory systems: the eye (vision) and the ear (hearing and balance).

Core concepts

  • The eye: Light is focused by the cornea and the lens, and lands on the retina. The retina contains rods (dim light, black and white) and cones (colour).
  • The ear: Outer, middle (eardrum and tiny bones) and inner (cochlea for hearing, semicircular canals for balance).
HIM FOCUS: PATHOLOGIES & TERMINOLOGY
  • Myopia: Short-sightedness (near is clear, far is blurry).
  • Hyperopia: Long-sightedness (far is clearer, near is blurry).
  • Presbyopia: Age-related loss of near focusing, caused by the lens stiffening. It is not the same condition as hyperopia.
  • Cataract: Clouding of the lens. Very common in older patients; treated by surgical lens replacement.
  • Glaucoma: A group of eye diseases involving optic nerve damage, often (but not always) with raised intraocular pressure. The "silent thief of sight."
  • Ocular hypertension: Raised intraocular pressure without optic nerve damage. A different diagnosis from glaucoma.
  • Otitis media: Middle ear infection. A leading reason children are prescribed antibiotics.
  • Hearing loss: Conductive (sound is blocked in the outer or middle ear) or sensorineural (problem in the cochlea or nerve pathway). Presbycusis is age-related sensorineural loss, usually high-frequency.
  • Tinnitus: Ringing or buzzing in the ears.
CHECKPOINT QUESTION 1

Scenario: A chart states: "Patient has difficulty hearing high-pitched sounds, likely due to ageing." What must the provider document before you can assign a specific type of hearing loss, and what do you do if it is missing?

Answer: The provider must document the type of hearing loss and its cause (for example, sensorineural hearing loss due to presbycusis). A coder cannot work out the type from the symptoms. If it is missing, you query the provider. Also note the wording "likely": uncertain terms such as likely, probable or suspected are treated differently in inpatient and outpatient coding, so check your coding guidelines.

CHECKPOINT QUESTION 2

Scenario: One chart says "clouding of the lens." Another says "elevated intraocular pressure." What does each point to, and what must you do before assigning a diagnosis for the second?

Answer: The first describes a cataract. The second is a finding, not a diagnosis: it could be ocular hypertension or glaucoma. You cannot decide this yourself. Code what the provider documents, and query if the diagnosis is not stated.

4. The Endocrine System (Tortora Ch. 18)

The system of hormones and glands. It uses chemical messengers to regulate metabolism, growth, reproduction and mood, relying heavily on negative feedback: when a hormone level gets too high, the body signals the gland to reduce production.

Core concepts

  • Pituitary gland: The "master gland" in the brain. It directs other glands.
  • Thyroid gland: In the neck. Controls metabolic rate.
  • Pancreas: Produces insulin (lowers blood sugar) and glucagon (raises it).
HIM FOCUS: PATHOLOGIES & TERMINOLOGY
  • Diabetes mellitus: A major coding challenge.
    • Type 1: The pancreas makes little or no insulin (autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing cells).
    • Type 2: The body's cells resist insulin, often alongside reduced insulin production over time.
    • HIM alert: ICD-10-CM uses combination codes for diabetes. The type, the body system affected and the complication are captured together (for example, Type 2 diabetes with diabetic neuropathy), but only when the provider documents the complication.
  • Hyperthyroidism: Overactive thyroid (weight loss, fast heart rate, heat intolerance).
  • Hypothyroidism: Underactive thyroid (weight gain, fatigue, cold intolerance).
  • Goitre: Abnormal enlargement of the thyroid gland.
CHECKPOINT QUESTION

Scenario: The physician documents: "Type 2 diabetes mellitus. Patient reports numbness and tingling in both feet." Can you code diabetic neuropathy? What do you do?

Answer: No. Numbness and tingling are symptoms, not a diagnosis. Neuropathy is a plausible clinical link, so you send a query asking the physician to clarify. Once "diabetic neuropathy" is documented, the combination code applies.

5. Review Quiz

Test yourself across all four systems. Remember: your job is to translate clinical language into standardised data, and to query when it is unclear.

QUESTION 1 (TERMINOLOGY)

Break down the word osteoarthritis. What do the word parts mean, and is the condition mainly inflammatory?

Answer: Osteo (bone) + arthr (joint) + itis (inflammation). The word part says inflammation, but OA is mainly a degenerative condition, which is why it is distinct from rheumatoid arthritis.

QUESTION 2 (NERVOUS SYSTEM)

If a patient has a haemorrhagic CVA, what is happening in the brain?

Answer: A blood vessel has ruptured and there is bleeding in or around the brain. This differs from an ischaemic CVA, where a blockage cuts off blood flow.

QUESTION 3 (SENSES)

Why must a coder not assign glaucoma just because the chart says "elevated intraocular pressure"?

Answer: Raised pressure is a finding, not a diagnosis. It may be ocular hypertension (a different diagnosis) or glaucoma, and glaucoma can even occur with normal pressure. The provider must document the diagnosis. If they have not, you query.

QUESTION 4 (ENDOCRINE)

What is the difference between Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes regarding insulin?

Answer: In Type 1 the pancreas makes little or no insulin because of autoimmune destruction. In Type 2 the cells resist insulin, and production may also fall over time.

A final note for HIM students. You will not perform surgery or prescribe medication. You will read an operative report and extract the exact anatomical location and pathology to assign a code. Master the terminology, know the difference between similar-sounding conditions, and know when to query. Confirm with your lecturer which coding system your course uses.
